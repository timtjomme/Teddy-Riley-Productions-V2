Harlem

1967–1984

1967 Born in Harlem +−

Born Edward Theodore Riley and raised in Harlem's St. Nicholas Houses, surrounded by church music, family, block parties, and a new sound called hip-hop starting to take shape on the streets around him.

1972 A five-year-old musician +−

By age five he's already picking out music on instruments around the house — an early sign of the ear and instinct that family and neighbors would soon start to notice.

1980 The first keyboard +−

At twelve, a Casio CZ-10 becomes his first keyboard — he takes it everywhere. Playing in the Universal Temple church band teaches him keys and several other instruments.

1983 Kids at Work +−

At sixteen, manager Gene Griffin brings Teddy into Kids at Work — his first step out of neighborhood performances and into the record business.

1984 First studio album +−

Kids at Work's self-titled debut — including "Sugar Baby," "Singing Hey Yeah," and "She's My Baby" — gives Teddy his first real time in a recording studio and his first taste of local success.

New Jack Swing

1985–1989

1985 "The Show" breaks through +−

Still a teenager, Teddy works on "The Show" with Doug E. Fresh and the Get Fresh Crew. It becomes one of hip-hop's foundational records and the real start of his production career.

1986 The sound takes shape +−

Kool Moe Dee's "Go See the Doctor" pairs Teddy's hard drum programming with musical hooks and becomes a street hit. He later points to this record as the moment New Jack Swing first fully clicked into place.

1986 Rooftop Records +−

Rooftop Records opens its doors above Harlem's Rooftop skating rink. Still a teenager, Teddy is put in charge of production and teaches himself the new generation of drum machines and samplers by trial and error.

1987 London & "I Want Her" +−

A recording session in London with Kool Moe Dee widens Teddy's technical range. Back home that same year, he writes and produces Keith Sweat's "I Want Her" — his first Billboard Hot 100 Top 5 hit, and New Jack Swing's first big national moment.

1988 Guy changes R&B +−

Guy's platinum debut fuses gospel-rooted vocals with hip-hop's bounce. "Groove Me," "Teddy's Jam," "I Like," and "Piece of My Love" set a new template that R&B would follow for years.

1988 "My Prerogative" +−

Released that October, Bobby Brown's "My Prerogative" carries Teddy's sound straight into the pop mainstream and cements his reputation as the architect behind New Jack Swing.

1989 Hip-hop hitmaker +−

Teddy stays busy on the hip-hop side too, producing for Kool Moe Dee and Big Daddy Kane, while Guy's "My Fantasy" — from Spike Lee's Do the Right Thing — tops the R&B chart.

Going worldwide

1990–1998

1990 Freedom & The Future +−

Teddy parts ways with manager Gene Griffin. Guy's second album, The Future, plays like a statement of independence and reaches No. 1 on the R&B/Hip-Hop Albums chart.

1991 Michael calls +−

Michael Jackson calls Teddy in to help shape Dangerous. Teddy steps away from Guy, brings in dozens of tracks, and begins an intense collaboration that will take New Jack Swing global.

1991 Dangerous +−

Teddy co-produces seven tracks on Dangerous, among them "Remember the Time," "Jam," "In the Closet," and the title track — his street-level rhythm polished to a worldwide pop shine.

1991 Virginia Beach future +−

Teddy relocates to Virginia Beach and builds Future Recording Studios — a creative home base that will go on to help raise the next generation of producers to come out of the city.

1992 Bobby, round two +−

Working out of Future Studios, Teddy reunites with Bobby Brown for the album Bobby, pushing the New Jack Swing sound further after their "My Prerogative" breakthrough.

1993 Blackstreet begins +−

Teddy forms Blackstreet and starts work on their debut album, building a new vocal group around his production from the ground up.

1994 Blackstreet arrives +−

Blackstreet's self-titled debut arrives, with "Before I Let You Go" among its lasting songs — the start of the second great group chapter in Teddy's career, after Guy.

1996 "No Diggity" +−

"No Diggity," featuring Dr. Dre and Queen Pen, becomes a No. 1 hit and a cultural landmark. The Another Level album turns Blackstreet into an international arena act.

1998 Grammy victory +−

Blackstreet take home the Grammy for Best R&B Performance by a Duo or Group with Vocals for "No Diggity," recognition for a record that had already become bigger than the charts.

Legacy

2000–2024

2000 A needed pause +−

After years without a real break, Teddy steps back from the road and the studio grind and moves to California to spend time with family, just as the record business starts turning digital.

2001 Invincible reunion +−

Teddy reunites with Michael Jackson for Invincible, contributing to "Heaven Can Wait," "2000 Watts," "Don't Walk Away," and "Whatever Happens."

2003–04 Blackstreet, Britney & LL +−

Blackstreet release Level II, and Teddy branches out further — producing Britney Spears's hit remake of "My Prerogative" and LL Cool J's "I'm About to Get Her."

2007 Honors around the world +−

A VH1 Hip Hop Honors tribute at the Hammerstein Ballroom is followed by sold-out shows in Japan and a New Year's Eve concert in Taiwan — proof of how far the catalog had traveled.

2009–11 New Jack goes K-pop +−

Teddy becomes one of the first American producers to shape the sound of South Korean pop, working with acts including Girls' Generation and Jay Park and bringing New Jack Swing to a new generation of listeners.

2012 Atlanta & the ICON Award +−

Back in the U.S. and now based in Atlanta, Teddy receives the ASCAP ICON Award along with a city proclamation, while mentoring and working alongside a new wave of Southern talent.

2016–17 Legend status, Guy reunited +−

The Soul Train Music Awards hand Teddy their Legend Award. The following year, Guy get back together for a sold-out Legends Tour, putting the original trio in front of old and new fans alike.

2018 Homecoming honors +−

Harlem hands Teddy the Key to the City and declares a New Jack City Day in his honor. On the Grammy stage that same year, Bruno Mars name-checks him as one of the teachers behind 24K Magic.

2019 6405 Hollywood Boulevard +−

On August 16, Teddy is awarded the 2,670th star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame — official recognition for more than three decades of work across R&B, hip-hop, and pop.

2020 Verzuz breaks the internet +−

A lockdown-era Verzuz matchup against Babyface turns into a multigenerational celebration of both catalogs. A glitch-heavy first attempt only builds anticipation for the rematch, which draws millions of viewers.

2022–24 A living legacy +−

The National Museum of African American Music honors Teddy at its Legends Luncheon, while a new generation keeps finding his records — sampling, streaming, and covering songs cut decades earlier.
