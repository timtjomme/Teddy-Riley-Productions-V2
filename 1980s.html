1980's

The 1980s: Where It Started

Harlem, New York. A teenager who'd been playing instruments in church since he was five years old was about to change the sound of American music.

Teddy Riley cut his teeth as a DJ and producer in the New York club scene before landing his first major credit at just 17, producing "The Show" for Doug E. Fresh & The Get Fresh Crew — one of the most influential hip-hop records of its era. From there, the momentum never let up. In 1986, he produced Kool Moe Dee's "Go See the Doctor," sharpening a style that pulled hip-hop's beats and attitude into R&B's pocket.

Then came 1987, and Keith Sweat's debut album Make It Last Forever. The record was a turning point — for Sweat, and for Riley. Its sound was something the industry hadn't heard before: the swing and shuffle of jazz, the rhythmic drive of hip-hop, the vocal richness of gospel and soul, all layered into a single production. The Village Voice put a name to it that year: new jack swing. "New jack" for the new kid changing the game; "swing" for the rhythm underneath it all.

By the late '80s, Riley wasn't just producing the sound — he was performing it. He formed Guy alongside Aaron Hall and Timmy Gatling, and the group's self-titled 1988 debut became a blueprint for a genre, translating everything Riley had been building in the studio into a defining act of its own.

It was the decade that took a Harlem kid from church pews and street corners to the center of a musical movement — one that would go on to shape Michael Jackson, Bobby Brown, Heavy D & the Boyz, and a generation of artists still ahead. The 1980s weren't just where Teddy Riley got his start. They're where new jack swing was born.
